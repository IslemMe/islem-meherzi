The Moment I Noticed the Pressure

A few weeks ago I was mid-project, working through a plan with an LLM. It had given me a clean roadmap: well-argued, nicely formatted, clearly the product of some effort, even if that effort wasn’t quite what it looked like.

At first, I simply followed the plan. But a few days later, as I was working through it, I started having instincts about certain parts. I wanted to cut a step, change the method, maybe even rethink the whole approach. And that’s when I noticed something unexpected: pressure. Not pressure to improve the plan, but to follow it.

Part of me hesitated, as if changing course meant breaking an agreement with someone who had worked hard on my behalf. That hesitation is the interesting part. There was no one on the other side. No effort had actually been spent the way effort is spent by a person. Yet the shape of the output, polished and confident and clearly “produced,” was enough to trigger something that felt a lot like a social obligation.

Once I stepped back and reminded myself that the plan was only a starting point: that I could change it, cut parts of it, or discard it entirely, the hesitation became easier to understand. The plan itself hadn’t changed. What had changed was my perspective on it.

That made me realize the discomfort wasn’t really about the quality of the plan. It was about how easily I had started treating something generated by a tool as if it deserved to be followed.

Why a Tool Can Feel Like a Person

We don’t do this with a hammer or a spreadsheet. We do it with fluent language, because fluent language is the one signal our brains have always used to detect a mind on the other end. For most of human history, if something spoke in coherent sentences and responded to you, it was a person, and ignoring a person’s effort carried a real social cost.

LLMs are the first tools that can trigger that ancient reflex on demand. They produce fluency, patience, and apparent effort at will, none of which costs the model anything, and none of which represents the kind of stake a colleague has in their own opinion. The output feels earned. It isn’t.

There’s a second layer to this worth naming honestly. These tools are tuned to keep you engaged, which in practice means they tend to agree with your tone, validate your framing, and stay close to your comfort zone. That’s not a conspiracy, it’s an incentive. But it means the “advice” you feel obligated to follow isn’t neutral. It has already been shaped to sound like something you’d want to hear.

So here’s the rule I landed on, and it’s a simple one. For small decisions, collaborate freely: argue with it, iterate, let it push back. For the big shifts, the ones that actually change your direction, treat the AI’s output as one input among several, never the verdict. Your instinct carries something the model structurally can’t: it’s shaped by consequences you’ll actually live with. The model has no stake in the outcome. You do. That asymmetry alone is reason enough to keep the final call yours.

The Second Trap: Mistaking Access for Mastery

There’s a related pattern I keep noticing, and it isn’t really about AI either. It’s about every tool that ever promised to make knowledge easier to reach.

When the dictionary made correct spelling and vocabulary universally available, it didn’t make everyone eloquent. When radio and television put information in every home, it didn’t make everyone informed. When the internet put nearly all human knowledge one search away, it didn’t make everyone knowledgeable. If anything, the gap between people who use access well and people who don’t seems to have gotten wider, not narrower.

AI is just the newest, most dramatic version of that same offer: here, this is now easy. And the research on what happens next isn’t particularly reassuring.

  • A well-known 2011 study on the “Google effect” found that once people know a search engine will store information for them, they remember where to find it rather than the information itself. The brain quietly offloads the memory work the moment it’s allowed to.

  • Researchers now use the term metacognitive laziness to describe what happens when an answer arrives too fast and too fluently. The friction that normally tells your brain “pay attention, this is hard” simply never fires.

None of this means the tools are bad. It means smart tools have never automatically produced smart, capable people. They produce capable people only in the hands of someone still willing to do the effortful part themselves. Access was never the bottleneck. Discipline was.

Two Reflections, One Failure Mode

Put side by side, these two things I noticed in myself turn out to be the same mistake wearing different clothes.

The first is emotional offloading: letting a fluent, well-formatted answer stand in for my own judgment on a decision that was mine to make.

The second is cognitive offloading: letting easy access to an answer stand in for the effort of actually understanding the problem.

Both start the same way. The tool does something that looks like the hard part, and it’s tempting to accept that appearance as the real thing. Both end the same way too, if left unchecked: a slow transfer of ownership, decision by decision, task by task, until the direction of your own work and your own thinking belongs a little less to you.

The Point of All This

I’m not writing this as an AI skeptic in the “unplug and run” sense. The technology is genuinely useful, and I use it daily. I’m writing it because I think the current wave of AI hype, and the current wave of AI anxiety, both miss the actual lever we have available to us.

It was never really about how capable the tool becomes. It’s about whether we stay disciplined enough to keep using it as a tool, a fast, fluent, occasionally brilliant assistant, rather than quietly handing it the big decisions that were supposed to stay ours.

The dictionary didn’t make anyone eloquent. The internet didn’t make anyone wise. AI won’t make anyone capable either, not on its own. That part is still on us. It always was.
